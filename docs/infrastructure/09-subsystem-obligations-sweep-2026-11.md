# Subsystem obligations sweep (November 2026)

The [platform operations audit](./08-platform-operations-audit-2026-11.md) looked at the job **machinery** and at every registered job. This page looks the other way round: for each of the 28 subsystems in the root `README.md`, it lists every **deferred obligation** (something that must happen later or repeatedly without a user acting: an expiry, a reminder, a hold and its release, a vendor retry, a renewal, a retention delete, a reconciliation, a statutory deadline) and names its **enforcer**: a registry job, a Postgres trigger or constraint, an inline request path, or nothing. It was written from read-only sweeps of `dev` at `827f0ce85` on 2026-11-11, plus web research on how mature teams build this. Findings already on page 08 (IDs M, J, D, C) are referenced, not repeated.

> **Status before launch.** The scheduled fleet is paused (`paused-prelaunch`), so every job finding below is latent until the fleet resumes. Several findings are not about jobs at all: they are read-time checks or back-office doors that are missing today.

## 1. The answer in one paragraph

The job **set** is nearly complete: across all 28 subsystems the sweep found only a handful of obligations with no enforcer at all, and almost every fix is a change to an existing job, a read-time check, a gauge in `lib/cron/pending-ages.ts`, or a back-office door, not a new registry job. The job **contract** is what is missing. The same four defects recur in most subsystems: a bounded read whose poison rows re-head the cohort forever, eligibility decided in JavaScript after the `LIMIT`, no attempt counter or quarantine, and no "work left over" signal. Fixing that contract once fixes most of the list. A few findings are real money or compliance defects that should land before launch (section 3).

## 2. How mature teams do this (research summary)

The research compared the current design with Postgres queues (pg-boss, Graphile Worker, River, Rails 8's Solid Queue) and durable-execution engines (Temporal, DBOS, Inngest, Trigger.dev, Hatchet, Vercel Workflow), using primary sources (engineering blogs, vendor docs, the AWS Builders' Library, the SRE Workbook).

**Verdict: keep the shape, add the contract.** A clock plus a Postgres-backed claim plus idempotent handlers is what every Postgres queue is underneath; the sweeps are level-triggered reconcilers, the most failure-tolerant form, because a missed tick is harmless. Hosted engines add little at this scale and most fail India residency: Trigger.dev's announced regions are US and EU, no Inngest India region was found, and only Temporal Cloud has Mumbai. DBOS (state in our own Postgres) or Vercel Workflow (after the `bom1` move) are the options if a multi-step flow with day-long waits ever appears.

**The sweep contract** every recovery job should follow:

| Part                                                                        | What it means                                                        | Fixes                                                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Attempt counter + `nextAttemptAt` with backoff                              | A failing row steps out of the head of the read                      | J1–J3, J7, J11, J13, BK-1, BK-7, MO-1.1, MO-3.3, BT-06 |
| Quarantine state + an alarm whenever it is non-empty                        | A row that exhausted its attempts is visible, never silently dropped | CC-2, BT-09, MO-3.1                                    |
| Eligibility in SQL, never in JavaScript after `LIMIT`                       | The bounded read returns only real work                              | J1, MO-4.3                                             |
| A partial index on the due predicate                                        | The read stays cheap as history grows                                | —                                                      |
| A row limit **and** a time budget per slice; loop until the budget is spent | One run does as much as it safely can                                | BK-3, CC-7, CC-9, J8                                   |
| `remaining` and `oldestDueAgeSec` on every run row                          | A backlog is visible before a customer notices                       | J8, CC-7, BT-07                                        |

**Deferred obligations stay on the domain rows.** A generic timer table or a workflow per booking duplicates state Postgres already holds, needs cancel-on-transition writes and still needs a reconciler. The recommendation is to keep per-domain due columns, standardise their shape, and add one read-only SQL view over all of them for observability. Use a timer table only if reminders with several user-set offsets appear.

**Throughput reality.** At about 60 ms per query from Singapore to Mumbai, three queries per item and a 20 s budget, one slice handles about 110 items; set-based writes give 10–50x more. At 100x scale the limit is the round trip, which is the existing Vercel `bom1` hosting trigger, not Postgres.

## 7. How this page was produced

Five read-only subsystem sweeps (identity and discovery; booking and checkout; money and digital products; communication and content; business relationships and trust) and one web research pass on 2026-11-11. The working reports are not committed; each finding was checked against `dev` by its sweep. These were re-checked by hand before this page was written: BT-01, BT-04, BK-6, CC-1, CC-3, I-1, MO-4.2 and MO-5.1. ## Deprecated & Superseded Approaches

- **A machinery-only audit.** Page 08 audited the scheduler, the leases and each registered job, and drew its missing-jobs list from a generic checklist. This page replaces that list with one derived from each subsystem's own lifecycle; page 08 §5.5 stays as the cross-cutting list.
- **Hand-tuned per-job bounds without a contract.** Each job chose its own limit, ordering and error handling, which is how the same starvation defect appeared in many subsystems. The sweep contract in section 2 supersedes that.
